OPERATION FULL SWING

BROKEN-FRACTALS
UNKNOWN
2571

Everyone here was sick. Fractals knew this about himself, sure; knew it about the other subjects sitting in the chairs around him, hooked up to IVs and monitors. He watched the translucent fluid drip, drip, drip down and into the needle hooked into the back of his hand.

Sick.

But it wasn’t just the people in the row of chairs lined up against the stark white wall. Disease had wormed its way into everyone here. The staff were impassive as they checked his vitals, clinical as someone down the line choked and spasmed for minutes that felt like hours. Cold as they pronounced time of death, called in aides that loaded them onto a stretcher and wheeled them away to be cut open, pulled apart, and brought back for the next round of tests. That would be him, soon. He knew how this went. But for the time being, he was bored. When had waiting for death become *boring*?

Murmuring reached his straining ears, and he shifted his gaze to look over at the monitoring station. Terminals cast the faces of his overseers in sickly shades of green and blue, and he noticed that something in their expressions had shifted. The masks of practiced neutrality had fallen, turning their gazes sharp and hungry. He caught the rustle of a page turning, and glanced toward the stack of magazines on the table beside his chair. Generally he ignored them, finding even the mind-numbing boredom of the wait preferable to writeups about celebrities he didn’t care about or political pieces so laced with corporate propaganda that the advertisements run alongside them carried more journalistic merit. But he hadn’t seen that look before, not in all of his time as a NanoTrasen lab rat, and that made it worth sifting through the drivel for whatever blood had started the sharks to circling.

“Top Ten—” No.
“This Week’s—” No.

“Cybersun Breach of Accord 2546 Prompts Retaliation, Jackstadt-Derring System to Transition to NanoTrasen Oversight”

The newspaper was weeks old. Print media had died long ago, lingering only for collectors and those sorry few who could not be allowed unfettered access to even internal networks. People like him, squirreled away in a facility so deep in secure territory that he was certain that not even their competitors knew it existed, a damned impressive feat in an age where information was more plentiful than oxygen in many sectors. He skimmed the article at first, then, when the reality of what he was reading hit him, started over and picked through it word by word.

A whole planet. Civilians. Nuclear warheads. Terraforming. Promised eradication of lingering Cybersun cells.

He scoffed. The report was drowning in sugarcoated corpo-speak, promising a fresh start for the system, but it was no different from what they had promised him, was it? Testing medicine, they had said. Fairly compensated for the risk. Housing coverage. A new start. It had been none of those things.

Another down the line lurched forward, fingertips scrabbling at the arms of their chair. This one was faster, at least.

What they were testing was poison. They were never going to pay him enough to cover the mountain of medical debt that had him in a stranglehold, that had dragged his family down into a mire of indentured servitude poorly disguised as corporate contract work. A flimsy cube of an apartment with two rooms, too-thin walls, and prepacked meals delivered once weekly. No escape.

A metallic taste crept up from his throat, and the first cough that came with it spattered crimson across the grey-brown paper. The second escaped as a laugh, and he wheezed as his guts spasmed and pain crawled dull-sharp-dull-twist-tear through his torso. This was where it ended, this time. Where it would end time and time again, until his data decayed and he stopped being useful.

This is where it ended.

It was never going to end.
